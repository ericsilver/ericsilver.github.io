Panic, Neglect, and Paperwork

Eric Silver · September 9, 2026 · draft

AI edited ideated supported. The shaded “AI background” blocks below are research notes drafted by an AI and checked against their sources; the argument is mine.

In the early 1970s New York City had a tuberculosis program that worked, and by the end of the decade it had almost nothing, because the program had worked. Cases had fallen for decades, the federal categorical grant that paid for TB control was eliminated in 1972 and not restored for nine years, the city's own fiscal crisis cut the health department's budget by a fifth, and six of its fourteen chest clinics closed. Cases bottomed out in 1978 and then climbed for fourteen years, to a peak in 1992 of nearly three times the low, with a strain of multidrug-resistant disease that had not existed when the clinics were open. Bringing it back under control cost the city and the federal government on the order of a billion dollars. The cheap program had been cut because it was cheap and because it was working; the expensive program was bought because the cheap one was gone.

That story is not a story about tuberculosis. It is the shape of most public problems I have worked on, and I want to write it down once, generally, and then complicate it three times. The general shape: a need becomes visible, the response over-shoots, the need recedes, the response is cut back because the need has receded, and the need returns. The complications: each turn leaves rules behind that no one clears; some “fixes” work by making the need invisible rather than smaller, and the people who fight about the need are not the people who vote on it.

The thermostat with a lag

Political scientists have a name for the ordinary version of this. Christopher Wlezien called the public a thermostat: when spending on a program goes up, the share of people who say we should spend more goes down, and vice versa. The public does not have a demand for a level of spending so much as a demand for a direction, and it keeps signalling “more” until it senses “enough,” then signals “less.” That is a well-behaved feedback loop. It becomes ill-behaved in exactly the way an engineer would predict, when there is a delay between the signal and the effect. A furnace that responds to the thermostat a day late overshoots, then undershoots, and the house oscillates. Anthony Downs described the same thing from the other end in 1972 as the issue-attention cycle: a problem is discovered with alarm, the public is euphoric about solving it, the public learns what solving it costs, interest declines, and the problem enters a “post-problem” twilight in which the institutions built at the peak persist while attention moves on. Baumgartner and Jones later formalized the alternation of long stability and short bursts as punctuated equilibrium. The furnace metaphor adds only one thing to those accounts, but it is the thing this essay is about: the oscillation is not a failure of will or attention. It is what a loop with lag does.

The lag has two halves. On the way up, the need is real long before it is legible: unsheltered people accumulate for years before a city calls it a crisis; a pathogen circulates for months before there is a count. On the way down, the absence of the need is legible immediately, but the reason for the absence is not. When TB cases fall, the budget line for TB control looks, to anyone reviewing budgets, like money spent on a problem we no longer have. Geoffrey Rose called the medical version of this the prevention paradox: a measure that brings large benefits to the population offers little to each individual, and so nobody is grateful. The fiscal version is worse. Prevention, when it works, produces nothing at all, and nothing is very hard to defend in a budget hearing.

The electoral evidence is blunt. Healy and Malhotra looked at natural disasters and found that voters reward incumbents for relief spending after a disaster and do not reward them for preparedness spending before one, even though a dollar of preparedness prevents roughly fifteen dollars of future damage in their data. A politician who reads that paper and behaves rationally will under-invest in preparedness and over-invest in relief, and the loop will oscillate with the politician's help. I do not think this is cynicism. It is the thermostat working through the only actuator it has.

AI background: the literature on policy cycles AI supported

Several literatures describe the same oscillation from different angles. In rough order of when they appeared:

  • Issue-attention (Downs 1972). “Up and Down with Ecology,” The Public Interest 28. Five stages: pre-problem; alarmed discovery and euphoric enthusiasm; realizing the cost of significant progress; gradual decline of public interest; post-problem. Downs's point was that the institutions built in stage two outlast the attention, which is the seed of the “cruft” complication below.
  • Prevention paradox (Rose 1981). “Strategy of prevention: lessons from cardiovascular disease,” BMJ 282. A preventive measure that brings large benefits to the community offers little to each participating individual. The budgetary corollary, that successful prevention has no visible output, is not Rose's but follows directly.
  • Punctuated equilibrium (Baumgartner & Jones 1993). Agendas and Instability in American Politics. Policy subsystems are stable for long stretches and change in bursts when an issue reaches the macro-political agenda; budgets show the same fat-tailed distribution of changes.
  • Thermostatic public (Wlezien 1995; Soroka & Wlezien 2010). “The Public as Thermostat,” American Journal of Political Science 39(4). Preferences for “more” spending fall when spending rises and rise when it falls; policy responds to preferences in turn. Degrees of Democracy (Cambridge 2010) extends the model across countries and domains.
  • Myopic retrospection (Healy & Malhotra 2009; Achen & Bartels 2016). “Myopic Voters and Natural Disaster Policy,” American Political Science Review 103(3): relief spending is electorally rewarded; preparedness spending is not, despite an estimated 15-to-1 damage-reduction ratio. Democracy for Realists (Princeton 2016) generalizes: voters punish incumbents for conditions, with limited attention to cause. Fowler and Hall (2018, Journal of Politics) dispute the shark-attack example specifically but not the broader pattern.
  • System dynamics (Forrester 1969; Sterman 2000). Urban Dynamics and Business Dynamics. Feedback loops with delays oscillate; the “beer game” is the classroom version. Forrester's controversial finding that low-cost housing programs in his model worsened city outcomes is an early example of an intervention that changes the loop's gain without changing its lag.
  • Panic and neglect (World Bank 2017). From Panic and Neglect to Investing in Health Security, the report of the International Working Group on Financing Preparedness, coined the phrase for the pandemic-finance version of the cycle three years before COVID-19.
  • Levee effect (White 1945). Human Adjustment to Floods. Flood-control works invite development onto the floodplain and raise the eventual loss when the works are overtopped. The engineering “fix” raises the stakes of the next cycle rather than ending it.

What is missing from all of these, and what this essay tries to add, is the interaction between the cycle and its residue: rules, visibility, and who shows up.

The cycle, a dozen times

I want to be tediously complete here, because the argument that the cycle is a property of the loop rather than of any one domain rests on how many domains it shows up in.

Malaria in Ceylon. A DDT spraying program begun after the war brought the count to seventeen cases in 1963. Spraying was halted that April because there was nothing to spray for. By 1969 there were more than half a million cases; mosquito resistance and a lapse in attention contributed, but the halt was the decision.

Pertussis in the 1970s. Whole-cell pertussis vaccine had made whooping cough rare enough that a vaccine-injury scare could be heard over the disease. In Britain coverage fell from 79 percent in 1973 to 31 percent in 1978, and the country had three epidemics in a row, the first of them with some five thousand hospital admissions and thirty-eight deaths. Japan went from fewer than four hundred cases and no deaths in 1974 to more than thirteen thousand cases and forty-one deaths in 1979. Sweden stopped vaccinating altogether. The disease had to come back before the vaccine was tolerated again.

Public health after 2008. American local health departments lost about a sixth of their staff in the decade after the recession, and the federal preparedness grants created after 2001 and the anthrax letters were cut by a quarter in nominal dollars between 2003 and 2021, roughly half in real terms. Then came 2020, and emergency appropriations that dwarfed the cuts, and now the cuts again.

The stockpile. The Strategic National Stockpile distributed some 85 million N95 respirators in the 2009 H1N1 pandemic and entered 2020 with about 13 million. They were not replaced. Its former director's explanation was that no funds arrived to replace them, which is to say that the pandemic ended.

The levees. The Army Corps' Southeast Louisiana flood control program was cut in the years before 2005, in part because nothing had flooded. Gilbert White had predicted the general form in 1945: the levee invites building behind it, so that when the cycle turns the loss is larger than it would have been without the levee.

The Bronx. New York closed or moved some fifty fire units in the 1970s on the recommendation of a RAND model that showed, correctly, that response times in most of the city would barely change. The companies closed were in the neighborhoods that then burned. The model measured the average and the fire happened in the tail.

The asylums. In 1955 the United States had about 340 public psychiatric beds per hundred thousand people; today it has around eleven. The 1963 Community Mental Health Centers Act promised fifteen hundred centers to replace the hospitals and built about half of them. The hospitals closed on schedule, the centers were never built, and the need moved to jails, emergency rooms, and sidewalks, where fifty years later it is being rediscovered with alarm.

The child tax credit. The 2021 expansion cut the child poverty rate, on the Census Bureau's supplemental measure, to 5.2 percent. It expired; the rate in 2022 was 12.4 percent. That is a full cycle in twenty-four months, with the useful property that nobody disputes the counterfactual.

Medicaid's continuous enrollment. From March 2020 to March 2023 states were paid not to disenroll anyone. Enrollment reached 94 million, its highest level ever, and the national uninsured rate fell to 7.7 percent, its lowest. Then the “unwinding”: more than twenty-five million people were disenrolled over the next year and a half, 69 percent of them not because they had been found ineligible but because a form was not returned. Some of them will be found again, at higher cost, in an emergency room.

Crime and policing. The 1994 crime bill funded a hundred thousand officers at the peak of an alarm; the alarm subsided as crime fell; in 2020 several large cities cut police budgets; by 2022 the same cities were restoring them. This one is unusual only in how fast it ran.

Roads, pipes, bridges. The deferred-maintenance literature is the same cycle stretched to the length of a concrete lifespan. Maintenance produces nothing visible; it is cut; the structure fails; there is a bond issue.

Pensions and trust funds. In good years, contribution holidays; in bad years, a shortfall that is now structural. New Jersey's pension system is the textbook case, but unemployment-insurance trust funds show the same waveform at every recession.

The domains have nothing in common except a loop with a lag. That is the point.

Complication one: the cruft

A furnace that oscillates at least returns to where it started. A policy loop does not, because each turn leaves something behind. On the way up, the response is legislated, and legislation is written in a hurry by people who have just been embarrassed by a problem and intend never to be embarrassed by it again. So it contains, along with the money, reporting requirements, oversight bodies, audit mandates, certification regimes, and definitions. On the way down, the money is cut. The reporting requirements are not, because cutting them costs political capital and saves almost nothing on any single line, and because the people who would cut them are the people who would be blamed if the problem recurred and it emerged that the reporting had been cut. The net effect is a ratchet: the program shrinks and its paperwork stays.

Herbert Kaufman asked in 1976 whether government organizations were immortal and found that of 175 federal agencies existing in 1923, all but a couple of dozen were still there fifty years later, most of them larger. Eugene Bardach and Peter deLeon spent the late 1970s trying to build a theory of policy termination and concluded, more or less, that it barely happens. Streeck and Thelen gave the mechanism a name: layering, in which new rules are added beside old ones because removing the old ones would require a coalition that no longer exists. Steven Teles's word for the accumulated result is kludgeocracy, a state that is not large so much as complicated, where the complication itself is the cost. Pamela Herd and Donald Moynihan measured that cost on the citizen's side as administrative burden: learning costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs, each of which falls hardest on the people the program was for.

Medicaid is where I have watched this most closely, and it is the best case I know because its rules accrete visibly, in the Federal Register, with dates. Every scandal has left a layer. The provider-tax and disproportionate-share games of the late 1980s left the DSH allotment rules and the provider-tax regulations. The 1990s managed-care migration left Part 438. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 left the Medicaid Integrity Program and a new class of audit contractors. The Affordable Care Act left provider screening and five-year revalidation. The 21st Century Cures Act left electronic visit verification for every home-care aide in the country. The 2016 managed-care rule ran to more than four hundred pages of the Federal Register; the 2024 rules on managed care and access added six hundred more, including a requirement that states demonstrate that 80 percent of what they pay for home-care services reaches the workers' compensation, which is a good idea and also a new report. The 2023 unwinding left monthly state reporting on renewals. None of the earlier layers were removed to make room.

What I wanted to know was the proportion. Of the effort that goes into overseeing a program like Medicaid, how much is invested in filing and how much in fixing? There is no ledger that answers that directly, so I built a crude one. The chart below classifies every document that the Medicaid agency, first the Health Care Financing Administration and then CMS, has published in the Federal Register since 1995: about seven and a half thousand of them. Each is sorted by its type and title into four bins. At the bottom, in orange, are rules and proposed rules whose subject is what the program does: payment, coverage, eligibility, conditions of participation. Above them, in blues, are rules whose subject is reporting, data, records, and disclosure; then Paperwork Reduction Act notices, which are the announcements that the agency intends to collect information from someone; and then the residual notices: meetings, hearings, quarterly listings of program issuances, accreditation approvals, privacy-system notices.

Read it however you like; the honest reading is that roughly four documents in five are about collecting or communicating information, and the share has drifted upward across thirty years and both parties. The Paperwork Reduction Act notices alone are the largest category in most years, and in every year since 2011, which is a joke the Act's authors did not intend. The orange band is not shrinking in absolute terms, because the agency is larger. It is shrinking as a share, because the filing grows faster than the fixing.

The chart counts documents, not effort, and a rule that changes hospital payment is not the same weight as a notice about a survey form. So let me add three measures of effort from inside the program, each of which points the same way.

First, the improper-payment machinery. CMS measures a Medicaid improper payment rate each year through a program called PERM, and the rate is reported to Congress as if it were a measure of money lost. In the years around the pandemic it was above 21 percent, then 15.6, then 8.6, then 5.1 percent. CMS's own explanation of the number, repeated in every fact sheet, is that the large majority of improper payments are not payments to ineligible people and do not indicate fraud; they are payments for which the paperwork was insufficient: an eligibility determination not documented the way the rule requires, a provider whose enrollment was not re-verified on schedule. In the 2024 measurement, 79 percent of the improper dollars were insufficient documentation; in 2023, 82 percent. When the eligibility-documentation component was reintroduced in 2019 after a pause, the rate rose from under 10 percent to above 20 without evidence that any additional dollar had gone to the wrong person. The national metric of Medicaid waste is, to a first approximation, a metric of filing.

Second, the audit contractors. The Deficit Reduction Act's Medicaid Integrity Contractors were the archetype of the layer left behind by a scandal. When the Government Accountability Office reviewed them in 2012, the national audit program had paid its contractors about a hundred million dollars and identified about seven million dollars in potential overpayments; of 1,550 audits, 4 percent found anything and 69 percent were discontinued or came back empty. The program was reorganized, which is the polite word, and the reporting requirements it had created for states remained.

Third, the part that does fix things. State Medicaid Fraud Control Units, which investigate and prosecute actual fraud and abuse, returned $3.35 for each dollar they cost in 2023 and $3.46 in 2024, on budgets under four hundred million dollars for the whole country, in the Inspector General's accounting. They are a rounding error against the forty-one billion dollars a year that Medicaid spends on administration. The proportion of Medicaid oversight that is invested in the activity with a positive return is small, and I have never seen anyone propose to change the proportion, because the reports are required and the investigators are discretionary.

Limitations, in order of importance. (1) Documents are counted, not pages or hours; a 1,400-page managed-care rule and a two-page survey notice each count once, which understates the substantive share by page and overstates it by hour. Sunsets that do not set

The obvious remedy has been tried. Colorado passed the first sunset law in 1976; within a few years most states had one. Texas's Sunset Advisory Commission is the survivor: six hundred reviews, forty-two agencies abolished outright and fifty-four more folded into others, and a claimed $1.1 billion in savings and revenue since 1985, which sounds large until it is set against forty years of Texas budgets. Richard Kearney's survey of the state experience in 1990 found that most sunset reviews had become pro forma and that a dozen states had already repealed or suspended their laws; by 2001 it was sixteen. The federal “one in, two out” order of 2017 removed few rules of consequence and none of the reporting requirements I have listed. Sunsets fail for the reason terminations fail: the review happens in the trough of attention, when the only people in the room are the people the rule employs.

What would land better, I think, is narrower. Do not sunset programs; sunset their reporting. Every information collection already carries an OMB control number and an expiration date; the renewals are the blue band in the chart, and they are approved at a rate indistinguishable from automatic. A rule that no collection may be renewed unless the agency shows what decision the collected information changed in the prior period would not shrink government by a dollar and would clear more of the cruft than any sunset law has. The test is not whether the data are used. Data are always used, for the next report. The test is whether anyone did anything differently because of them.

Complication two: fixes that hide

The thermostat only works if it can feel the temperature. A whole class of policy responses works not by lowering the need but by moving it out of the sensor's range. These are not always cynical; sometimes they are the only thing a city knows how to do. But they have a specific effect on the loop: they turn off the signal while the need is still rising, so that when it becomes visible again it is much larger and the response overshoots more.

Homelessness is the clearest case. An encampment sweep changes the count of people a commuter sees and does not change the count of people without housing. Since Grants Pass v. Johnson in June 2024 allowed cities to enforce camping bans regardless of shelter availability, more than three hundred and fifty cities and fourteen states have passed or expanded bans, and the national point-in-time count, which was 653,000 in 2023, 771,000 in 2024, and 746,000 in 2025, is now being measured against a rising tide of ordinances whose purpose is to make it smaller without housing anyone. The older version of the same move is the bus ticket: San Francisco's Homeward Bound program has sent about eleven thousand people out of the city since 2005, and a Guardian investigation counted thirty-four thousand such journeys across sixteen cities and counties in six years, with almost no one following up on where the passengers landed; San Francisco stopped recording destinations in 2022. Hostile architecture, the bench with the third armrest, is the same policy in cast iron. Each of these lowers the reading on the thermostat. None lowers the temperature.

Drug use runs the other way, and it is instructive. Oregon's Measure 110 decriminalized possession in February 2021, and the legislature recriminalized it in 2024 with polls showing about two-thirds of Oregonians in favor. The peer-reviewed studies that looked for an effect of decriminalization on overdose deaths, using other states as controls and adjusting for when fentanyl arrived, did not find one; fentanyl came to Oregon on its own schedule. What changed was visibility. Use that had been indoors and dispersed became outdoors and concentrated, and the public reacted to what it could see. The deaths, which were the actual harm, and which tripled in Oregon between 2019 and 2023, produced no comparable reaction because a death from an overdose is a private event with a paper record and a public drug scene is a public event with none. The thermostat is wired to the sidewalk, not to the morgue.

Taxes are the version economists have measured most carefully. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft put tax-inclusive price tags on a grocery shelf and watched demand for those items fall by about 8 percent, relative to identical items with the tax added at the register; the tax was the same, only its visibility had changed. Amy Finkelstein found that when toll roads adopted electronic collection, so that drivers stopped handing over cash, tolls ended up 20 to 40 percent higher than they would otherwise have been, because the price had stopped being felt. Cabral and Hoxby found property taxes higher where they are paid invisibly through mortgage escrow. Retailer-side levies, gross-receipts taxes, the corporate income tax, about half of which comes out of wages, and above all tariffs, which by one running estimate reached an effective rate of 17 percent in 2025 at a cost of more than two thousand dollars per household, are all taxes on people who do not see them. Puviani called the general phenomenon fiscal illusion in 1903 and Buchanan revived the term in 1967, and the finding has held up under every method since: the less a tax is felt, the higher it gets.

Here is where I want to be careful, because it is tempting to conclude that hiding is always wrong and visibility always right, and that is not what the loop says. Consider the opposite strategy, which has its own name. When the National Park Service's budget was cut in 1969, its director closed the Washington Monument and the Grand Canyon two days a week: the smallest possible saving, chosen for the largest possible outcry, which duly restored the budget. Charles Peters called it the “firemen first” principle: when threatened with a cut, propose to close the fire station. The 2013 sequestration ran the same play with a hundred and forty-nine air-traffic control towers and the White House tours; California's 2011 budget listed seventy state parks for closure, and sixty-nine of them were still open a year later, rescued by exactly the outcry the list was meant to produce. The principle is that the streetlights and the parks go first, precisely because everyone will notice. It is, in loop terms, a deliberate increase in the gain of the sensor: make the cut visible now so the cut is reversed now, rather than letting the need accumulate invisibly for a decade.

So visibility can be manipulated in both directions, and the question is not whether to manipulate it but where the honest reading is. My answer, problem by problem:

For homelessness, the visible count should not be the number the budget responds to, because it is the number most easily gamed, in both directions. The budget should respond to the inflow: the number of people entering homelessness in a year, which the homeless management information systems already record and which no sweep changes. That means accepting some visible disorder as the price of an honest signal, and it means saying so, which no elected official will do. I would settle for cities that publish inflow next to the point-in-time count and let the discrepancy speak.

For drugs, the honest reading is deaths, and the fix is to publish overdose deaths the way road deaths are published: monthly, by place, with the same prominence. A public that sees the number will still react to the sidewalk, but it will at least be able to tell whether a policy that cleared the sidewalk did anything for the number. In Oregon it did not; deaths fell in 2024, as they did nationally, before the new law took effect, and the state is now, in effect, back where it started with more rules.

For taxes, the answer is the least popular one: make them salient. Post prices with the tax included, as Chetty's grocery store did; print the employer's half of the payroll tax on the pay stub, whose incidence economists still argue about and workers never see; bill tariffs at the register. Every one of these lowers the revenue a given rate can raise, which is why no treasury does them. But a tax that raises money only because it is not felt is a tax whose level no one has chosen, and the thermostatic model says that level will drift upward until something visible breaks.

The threshold problem sits under all of this. People tolerate a great deal before reacting: the count of unsheltered people can double before it becomes a mayoral issue, a hidden tax can rise for a decade before anyone notices the receipt. That high threshold is why the cycle overshoots. And electability is why officials prefer the hidden version: a visible problem is blamed on the incumbent, an invisible one is not, so the incumbent's incentive is to hide until the threshold is crossed and then to overreact. I do not have a clean way out of that. I have the observation that every mechanism above, the inflow count, the death count, the tax-inclusive price, is a way of lowering the threshold without waiting for the crisis, and that lowering the threshold is the only intervention that damps the loop rather than shifting its phase.

Complication three: the people in the room

Everything so far has treated “the public” as a single sensor. It is not. The public that votes on a problem and the public that shows up to argue about it are different populations, and the difference is the last complication.

The median-voter theorem, from Hotelling by way of Duncan Black and Anthony Downs, says that in a two-party contest over a single dimension, both parties converge on the voter in the middle. It is the most-cited and least-observed result in political science. Bawn and her coauthors explained why: parties are coalitions of policy-demanding groups, and those groups exploit an “electoral blind spot,” the range of positions within which voters cannot tell or do not care how far a party has drifted from the median. Broockman and Skovron found that state legislators of both parties overestimate their constituents' conservatism, by a wide margin, because the constituents they hear from are not a sample of the constituents they have. The people who write to a legislator, comment on a rule, or speak at a hearing are self-selected by intensity, and intensity is not representative of anything.

The evidence on who shows up is consistent and a little bleak. Einstein, Glick, and Palmer coded thousands of public comments at planning meetings in Massachusetts and found that commenters were older, whiter, more likely to own their homes, and far more likely to oppose new housing than the voters of the same towns, who supported it in surveys. Yackee and Yackee found that the comments filed on federal rules come overwhelmingly from business and that agencies move toward them. Sarah Anzia showed that moving a school-board election off the November cycle lowers turnout to the point where organized groups, in her case teachers' unions, decide it, and that pay follows. Schattschneider's line about the pluralist heaven has been quoted so often it is invisible, and it is still true: the chorus sings with an upper-class accent, and it also sings with an advocate's accent, a homeowner's accent, and the accent of whoever has a professional reason to be in the room on a Tuesday afternoon.

This matters for the cycle in a specific way. The self-selected are the ones who keep a problem on the agenda in the trough, which is good, and who resist any cut to the response at the peak, which is not always good, and who are, in either phase, the constituency for the cruft, because they are the people who read the reports. Advocates are the reason the reporting layer never comes off: it is the only thing the trough leaves them.

So there is a case for institutions that privilege the median voter's judgment over the advocate's, and a real one. Fishkin's deliberative polls, the most careful version, take a random sample of citizens, give them balanced briefing materials and a weekend with experts, and measure what they think afterward. In “America in One Room” in 2019, five hundred and some randomly selected Americans moved substantially on a range of policy questions, generally toward the middle and toward each other. Ireland's Citizens' Assembly on abortion produced a recommendation the legislature had not been able to reach in thirty years and the electorate then ratified. The OECD counted nearly three hundred such deliberative processes by 2020. The idea in all of them is to build a sensor that reads the median instead of the loudest.

The objection is the one the user of any thermostat would raise: the median voter does not know the temperature. Kuklinski and his coauthors showed that Americans are confidently wrong about welfare, in the direction of thinking it larger and its recipients less deserving than they are, and that correcting the facts barely moves opinion. Gallup found in nearly every year from 1993 through 2024 that most Americans believed crime was rising nationally, including the years in which it fell by half. A median-voter institution consulting on homelessness will be consulting people who overestimate the share of the homeless who are addicted, underestimate the share who work, and have not read the inflow numbers. The advocates, whatever their biases, have. And the people who have actually been homeless, or on Medicaid, or in a state hospital, who now sit on the beneficiary advisory councils that the 2024 Medicaid access rule requires every state to convene, have a kind of knowledge the median voter cannot be briefed into.

Where I land is a division of labor rather than a choice. The median should set the level: how much of the public's money and tolerance a problem gets, which is the question a deliberative poll can answer well once the facts have been laid out, and the question advocates answer badly because they are advocates. The self-selected should set the design: what the money buys, in what order, with what rules, which is the question the median voter cannot answer and would not want to. The reporting layer is the place where this division is currently reversed: the median never sees the reports, and the advocates cannot let them go. A deliberative body asked, every few years, which of a program's reports it would be willing to lose would do more for the cruft than any sunset commission has, because its members have no professional reason to keep any of them.

This will not be comfortable for the advocates, and I count myself among them on most of the problems above. The honest version of the discomfort is that a process that consults the median will, in the trough, fund less than we want, and in the peak, fund less than we could get. That is the damping. It is what a loop that does not oscillate feels like from inside.

Four settings on the loop

Control theory has exactly one lesson for a loop that oscillates, and it has four parts. Lower the gain: do not respond to the peak with the largest response the moment allows, because the size of the overshoot is the size of the next cut. Shorten the lag: measure the need where it is, inflow rather than count, deaths rather than disorder, and publish it before the threshold is crossed. Add damping: trust funds, maintenance reserves, automatic stabilizers, anything that commits the trough to the peak's spending without a vote. And clean the residue every cycle: sunset the reports, not the programs, and let a body with no stake in the paperwork decide which of it to keep.

None of these is new. What I have wanted to show is that they are the same intervention seen from four sides, and that the alternatives, the sweep, the hidden tax, the audit contractor, the public comment period, are each a way of changing the loop's phase without changing its amplitude. The city's TB clinics were closed by a budget that could see their cost and not their output. Fifty years later Medicaid's oversight budget can see its reports and not its recoveries. The problem is the same problem. It is a sensor pointed at the wrong thing, and the fix is to point it.
