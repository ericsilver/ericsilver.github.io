Designing a Better Placebo

Eric Silver · August 24, 2026 · draft

A placebo is a substance defined entirely by what it does not do, and two different institutions want it to do that not-doing differently. The regulator wants a placebo that is inert to the point of invisibility: a control so free of its own effect that whatever the real drug does stands out cleanly against it. The clinic — or the patient, or the parent buying a chewable dextrose tablet for a child who has a stomachache and no diagnosis — wants the opposite: a placebo that works. Nobody has ever had to reconcile these, because nobody standardizes what a placebo is made of, how it is given, or by whom. This is an essay about what falls out when you look at the placebo as a product with an unregulated formulation, an undisclosed ingredient list, a marketing budget, and, on the evidence, wildly different quality control from one manufacturer, one country, and one clinician to the next.

The regulatory guidance that governs when a trial needs a placebo at all defines the standard directly: “assay sensitivity is a property of a clinical trial defined as the ability to distinguish an effective treatment from a less effective or ineffective treatment.”1 A placebo that worked too well would ruin the experiment it exists to run — if the sugar pill relieved as much pain as the drug, a trial could not tell an effective medicine from a useless one, which is why non-inferiority designs are treated with suspicion in the first place: two drugs can look equivalent because they are both good, or because neither is doing anything.1 Industry’s “better” placebo is a null result wearing a costume: identical in weight, color, smell, and packaging to the drug, and otherwise as close to nothing as chemistry allows.

Nobody checks whether it gets there. When investigators reviewed randomized trials published across 2008 and 2009 in four of medicine’s most prominent journals, the placebo’s composition was disclosed for pills in 8.2 percent of trials, and for injections and other treatments in 26.7 percent — not because pill placebos are simpler, but because nobody is required to say.2 “No regulations govern placebo composition,” the paper opens, and thirteen years later that sentence is still true: of 113 placebo trials published in 2016 in the same tier of journal, only 19.5 percent disclosed contents in the paper itself, and even the trial protocols — the internal planning documents, not the public-facing article — disclosed contents only 45.1 percent of the time; not one paper, and only four protocols, gave any reason for choosing the ingredients they used.3

It should matter, because the placebo you can’t see the ingredients of is not a fixed dose of nothing; it is itself a variable the trial has not controlled. Sixty duodenal-ulcer trials that measured healing endoscopically — a result nobody can talk themselves into — found placebo healed 44.2 percent of patients on a four-times-daily regimen against 36.2 percent on a twice-daily one, an eight-point gap driven by nothing but how often the patient was asked to swallow a pill that did nothing.4

It matters in every other dimension anyone has bothered to test. Volunteers told an inert pill was an expensive new painkiller reported relief 85 percent of the time; told the same pill was marked down to ten cents, 61 percent.5 A branded aspirin outperformed an identical unbranded one, and the gap was largest among placebo takers, not drug takers — branding sells hardest to the group with nothing else going for it.6 A placebo injected under the skin relieved migraine more often than an identical placebo swallowed.7 A sham acupuncture needle beat an inert pill on its way down, not just at the finish line — the ritual’s advantage showed up in the slope of recovery, not only the endpoint.8 And the ceiling case: a review of fifty-three trials that used sham surgery as the control found patients improved after the fake operation in seventy-four percent of trials, and in half of those the real operation did no better — the placebo effect of being cut open, closed up, and told it worked is large enough to erase what surgeons are for.9

Occasionally the placebo isn’t inert by accident. Olive and corn oil, both of which modestly lower cholesterol on their own, have served as the placebo in cholesterol-lowering trials — a control biased against the very drug it is meant to isolate.10 The comparator used in a string of Tamiflu trials contained dehydrocholic acid, a bile-acid compound that itself causes gastrointestinal upset, in a drug trial whose principal claimed benefit was gastrointestinal.11 A placebo built to be invisible has to be seen to be checked, and mostly nobody looks.

Even where composition is not the problem, arithmetic is. Most of what gets called “the placebo effect” is placebo response — improvement in the placebo arm of a trial — and that is not the same measurement as the placebo effect, because a placebo arm also captures the disease’s natural course, statistical regression to the mean, and the ordinary tendency of a patient asked “how are you” by someone who cares to say “better.” The only way to separate the two is a third arm that gets nothing at all, and when investigators pooled every trial built that way, placebo showed essentially no advantage over no treatment on outcomes anyone could measure objectively, and a real but modest advantage — worth about six and a half millimeters on a hundred-millimeter pain scale — confined almost entirely to pain and a handful of other subjective symptoms.12 The number that convinced two generations of doctors placebo was powerful, Henry Beecher’s 1955 finding that thirty-five percent of patients improved on placebo alone, was reanalyzed forty years later and found to contain “no evidence... of any placebo effect in any of the studies cited” — just spontaneous remission, symptom fluctuation, and patients being polite to the person asking.1314

And even the honest, subjective, well-measured remainder is not one number. A synthesis of a hundred and seventeen ulcer trials, run on the same class of drug in thirty-two countries, found placebo healing rates ranging from zero to one hundred percent, with the drug’s own effectiveness rising and falling in step with the placebo’s — as if the “active” ingredient and the sugar pill were both being carried by something the trial never measured.15 The country effect alone is large enough to answer a question worth asking directly: West German ulcer trials in that synthesis put placebo healing near sixty percent; trials from Brazil put it near seven.15 Nobody has identified what causes that gap — trial design, patient expectation, physician manner, and the cultural meaning of the ritual are all live candidates — but “the same placebo” plainly is not the same intervention on two sides of an ocean.

It isn’t even stable over time in the same country. In neuropathic-pain trials run in the United States between 1990 and 2013, the placebo arm’s response climbed considerably while the drug arm’s held flat, narrowing the gap a drug needs to clear to prove itself — and the rise appeared only in American trials, not elsewhere.16 Whether the same is true of antidepressants is contested: one widely cited 2002 analysis found placebo response rising with publication year across seventy-five trials,17 but the largest and most recent synthesis, covering two hundred and fifty-two trials and more than twenty-six thousand placebo patients back to 1978, found a level shift around 1991 and essentially flat response since.18 Even the claim that placebo is getting better at its job depends on which placebo, measuring which symptom, in which country, in which decade you ask about.

Which raises the two questions a trade publication would ask about any other product: which manufacturer makes the best placebo, and which makes the worst? Neither has an answer, and the reason is structural rather than a gap in the search. A specialty industry exists to manufacture “matched placebos” — tablets tooled to the exact dimensions of an active drug, liquids blended to its taste and smell, capsules over-encapsulated to hide a different pill inside — and its marketing sells fidelity of appearance, manufacturing compliance, and blinding integrity.19 What it does not sell, because no client is asking to buy it, is a comparative measure of inertness: nobody publishes a league table of which vendor’s placebo behaves most like nothing, because — see above — almost nobody discloses which vendor it was, or what they used, in the first place.

Whether nonprofit sponsors do any better is the same unanswerable question wearing a different hat; no comparison exists, because the disclosure gap that hides which company sold the placebo also hides which kind of company funded the trial that used it. Where placebo does have an open, competitive retail market, the marketing is refreshingly direct about what it’s selling. “Pure Honest Placebo,” reads the tagline for Zeebo, a brand of microcrystalline-cellulose capsules sold to consumers with the promise “you are the active ingredient” — candor as the entire product.20 Obecalp — “placebo” backward — sells cherry-flavored dextrose chewables to parents for children who complain of aches with nothing obviously wrong, explicitly outside FDA jurisdiction because it contains no medicine at all.21 Both brands found the same solution to the industry’s disclosure problem: disclose everything, and sell the honesty.

Set the manufacturers aside and look at what the clinic can actually control, which is not the pill but the performance around it, and there the literature is almost embarrassingly consistent: care itself titrates like a drug. In a trial that split irritable-bowel-syndrome patients three ways — a waiting list, a brief and businesslike sham acupuncture, or the identical sham delivered with warmth, attention, and voiced confidence in the treatment — relief climbed in a straight line: twenty-eight percent, forty-four percent, sixty-two percent, the top arm landing in the range of the condition’s actual drugs.22 The authors called it “a graded dose escalation,” and named the practitioner relationship as its single largest component.22 The effect isn’t only felt; it’s measured. A histamine-triggered skin welt, an outcome no patient can fake, shrank under a positive expectation only when the person delivering it was rated both warm and competent — cold or incompetent erased the benefit entirely.23 It even appears to be contagious: experimenters covertly led to believe an inert cream was a real analgesic transmitted lower pain reports, and lower skin-conductance readings, to the patients they treated, and researchers could trace the channel through the experimenter’s own face.24

This suggests a further question the studies above don’t quite answer: does it matter who is standing over the patient — their gender, their age, whether they look like the person receiving the treatment? No placebo trial has randomized concordance directly, so anything stronger than a proxy is speculation dressed as data. The closest real evidence sits one step outside placebo research entirely. An observational study of more than half a million heart-attack admissions in Florida found women treated by male physicians died more often than women treated by female physicians, with no such gap among male patients — suggestive, but not randomized, and contested since.25 A genuine field experiment came closer to causal proof for race: Black men randomly assigned a Black physician chose far more preventive care after the visit than those assigned a non-Black one, evidence the authors used to estimate that concordant care alone could close a fifth of the Black-white gap in cardiovascular mortality — a striking result, but about real medical advice, not a placebo.26 The one place gender concordance has actually been tested inside an experimental pain paradigm found men reported less pain to a female experimenter than a male one,27 and a careful follow-up found that effect lived entirely in what subjects said, not in their heart-rate variability or skin conductance — closer to a polite performance for the person in the room than a physiological response to them.28

The performance and the physiology are not the same claim, and the physiology, at least, is not in doubt. Placebo analgesia is blocked by naloxone, the drug that reverses opioid overdose, which means the relief runs through the body’s own opioid system and not merely through what the patient chooses to report.29 It shows up in the autonomic nervous system directly — placebo analgesia damps the heart-rate response to pain and quiets the same low-frequency heart-rate-variability band that beta-blockers target, and naloxone reverses both the pain relief and the change in heart rhythm together.30 It shows up as far down as the spinal cord, where placebo analgesia measurably reduces pain-signal activity before it ever reaches the brain to be reported at all.31 So: yes, you can see it in heart rate. What nobody has yet shown is that the identity of the person giving it moves that same physiological needle — only what the patient says about it.

Step back further, past medicine’s own rituals, to the largest “treatments” nobody can ethically randomize. Women in the Nurses’ Health Study who attended religious services more than once a week had thirty-three percent lower mortality over the follow-up period than women who never attended, with roughly half the effect explained by measurable mediators — more social support, less smoking, less depression, more optimism — and half not explained at all.32 A meta-analysis of a hundred and forty-eight studies and more than three hundred thousand people found stronger social relationships carried fifty percent higher odds of survival, an effect size in the range of other well-established mortality risks.33 Both findings share the placebo trial’s oldest problem in an incurable form: nobody can randomize a person into believing, or into having friends, so healthier people who were always going to live longer may simply be the ones who kept showing up — the confound is permanent, because the intervention cannot be blinded even in principle. Community, like care, may be a drug. It is also a drug that can never pass its own trial.

Which makes it worth asking what a placebo effect looks like in a patient who has no beliefs to confound. Rats given saccharin water paired once with an immune-suppressing drug later suppressed their own antibody response to saccharin alone, with no drug in it at all — a conditioned reflex reaching into the immune system, proof that a placebo-shaped response needs no belief, only a nervous system that has learned an association.34 Dogs are murkier: across the placebo arms of canine epilepsy trials, seventy-nine percent of dogs on placebo had fewer seizures than at baseline, which is probably regression to the mean and inattentive owner counting rather than any canine expectation.35 The clearest animal result puts the belief back on the human side of the leash: in the placebo arm of an osteoarthritis trial, owners rated their dogs improved forty percent of the time and veterinarians rated them improved nearly forty-five percent of the time, while a force platform measuring the dogs’ actual gait recorded meaningful improvement in seven of fifty-eight.36 The placebo effect in a dog is, more often than not, a placebo effect in the person watching the dog.

The word has been doing something like this work for eight hundred years before anyone tested a pill. “Placebo” — Latin for “I shall please” — opens the Vespers antiphon sung for the dead; mourners hired to sing it were said to be “singing placebo” for pay, and by Chaucer’s day the word meant a flatterer, the toadying courtier named Placebo in the Merchant’s Tale.37 The first physician recorded using it at the bedside was William Cullen, lecturing in Edinburgh in 1772, more than two centuries after the word already meant telling someone what they wanted to hear.37 The first blinded, sham-controlled trial in medical history was run not by a physician but by a royal commission — Franklin and Lavoisier among its members — sent by Louis the Sixteenth in 1784 to investigate Mesmer’s animal magnetism; blindfolded subjects convulsed at trees they were falsely told had been magnetized and sat calm before trees that actually had been, and the commission’s verdict was “imagination.”38 Sixteen years later an English physician tested Elisha Perkins’s patented metal healing rods against wooden fakes painted to look the same, and four of five rheumatic patients improved on the fakes — the same result the real metal rods produced the next day.37 The placebo has always worked roughly as well as its story, a strange thing for medicine to have discovered before it discovered penicillin.

Everything above has a mirror image, and the mirror is not gentler. Women in labor told before an epidural injection “you are going to feel a big bee sting; this is the worst part” rated the pain a five out of ten; women told “we are going to numb the area and you will be comfortable,” given the identical needle and the identical drug, rated it a three.39 Men taking finasteride who were warned it might cause sexual side effects reported them forty-four percent of the time; men taking the identical pill who were not warned reported them fifteen percent of the time.40 Even the price effect runs backward under the right framing: volunteers told an inert cream was expensive, rather than cheap, felt more pain from an identical stimulus, not less — the same brain regions that made an expensive placebo work better in the pain trials made an expensive nocebo hurt more, tracked all the way down the spinal cord.41 And belief needs no drug at all to manufacture harm from nothing: people primed to expect symptoms from wind-turbine infrasound reported worsening symptoms during a real infrasound exposure and an inaudible sham one alike, while people with no such priming reported nothing under either.42 The story is the active ingredient in both directions.

Some of what looks like belief doing the killing is really biology, and it is worth being honest about which is which before turning to the parts that are mostly belief. The 1918 influenza killed an estimated fifty to one hundred million people worldwide, a toll driven in real part by genuine immunological novelty — a virus the human population had no prior exposure to and no partial immunity against.43 The pattern repeats wherever a pathogen meets a population with no history of it: European diseases that were routine childhood illnesses at home were catastrophic “virgin soil” epidemics among Indigenous peoples in the Americas, who had never been exposed and had no partial protection to fall back on.44 Even a person’s own immune history can work against them: the first strain of flu anyone meets shapes how their antibodies respond to every strain after, sometimes for the worse — a phenomenon virologists call original antigenic sin.45 Novel pathogens really are, on average, more dangerous, and the reason has nothing to do with what anyone calls them.

The perception side of novelty is a separate claim, and the best-known study of it did not survive scrutiny. A 2014 paper argued that hurricanes with feminine names kill more people than hurricanes with masculine ones, because people take a “Hurricane Eloise” less seriously than a “Hurricane Charley” and prepare accordingly — a claim built on six decades of storm fatalities and six laboratory experiments.46 Independent statisticians who reanalyzed the same data found the model absurdly unstable — it predicted roughly twenty thousand deaths for Hurricane Sandy, which killed a hundred and fifty-nine — and found the headline effect vanished once a single missing interaction term was added back in, or once the analysis was restricted to storms named after 1979, when the US started alternating male and female names at all; before that year every Atlantic hurricane had a woman’s name, and pre-1979 storms were deadlier across the board because forecasting and warning systems were worse, which means “feminine” in the original data was partly just a proxy for old.47 What survived the reanalysis was the laboratory half: told to imagine identical storms, people really did rate the masculine-named ones as more dangerous and reported more intention to evacuate.46 The name changes how frightened people say they are. Whether it changes how many of them die is, on the honest reading of the evidence, still an open question the original paper did not answer.

Whoever names diseases has decided the stakes are real regardless. The World Health Organization’s 2015 guidance forbids naming a new disease after a place, an animal, a person, or a fear word — no Middle East, no swine, no unknown, no fatal — on the stated grounds that bad names “provoke a backlash against members of particular religious or ethnic communities, create unjustified barriers to travel, commerce and trade, and trigger needless slaughtering of food animals.”48 That last clause is not rhetorical: in 2009, on nothing more than the name “swine flu,” Egypt ordered the slaughter of essentially its entire pig population, some three hundred thousand animals, over the explicit objection of the WHO and the FAO, who had already said the virus was spreading person to person and had nothing to do with pigs.49 In 2022 the WHO renamed monkeypox “mpox” specifically to strip out “racist and stigmatizing language” attached to the old name — a name that had never even been very accurate, since the virus was identified in captive research monkeys, not because monkeys are its natural reservoir.50 None of this requires a new disease; the same label changes behavior around conditions everyone already has. Workers newly told they had hypertension, with no change in severity and no new treatment, took eighty percent more sick days the following year than before they knew.51 Parents shown a fussy, spitting-up infant took the diagnosis “GERD” as reason enough to want medication, even when told outright that the medication probably would not help.52 Patients told they had “heart failure” rated their prognosis worse and their mood lower than patients told, about the identical condition, that their heart “wasn’t pumping as hard as it should.”53 And in the starkest version, women shown one description of a real, unchanging breast lesion chose surgery over watchful waiting sixty-nine percent of the time when it was called an “abnormal cell growth,” sixty-six percent when it was called a “breast lesion,” and fifty-three percent — sixteen points lower, on the identical facts — when it was called, correctly and technically, a “noninvasive cancer.”54 The single word moved roughly one woman in six from surveillance to the operating table.

Given all that, has anyone tried the opposite — naming an illness something reassuring, on purpose, the way this essay’s title suggests, calling something Betty instead of a fever? No precedent turned up. The WHO’s actual doctrine runs the other way: modern disease and variant names are engineered to be as bland and forgettable as possible — COVID-19, mpox, the Greek-letter names briefly used for SARS-CoV-2 variants — specifically so that nothing memorable or human attaches to them for stigma to grab onto.48 The nearest real-world experiment in warm, human, easy-to-remember names for a dangerous thing is hurricanes, and it is the very naming convention the section above just watched fail to prove either dangerous or safe. A disease called Betty might inspire less dread. Given how directly the informed-consent and diagnosis-label findings above show that the amount of dread a name inspires is doing real clinical work — fewer side effects reported, fewer sick days taken, fewer operations chosen — it isn’t obvious a calmer name would be a better one.

Two structural facts round out the picture. Placebo response is not a trait some people carry and others lack so much as a capacity that can be switched off: Alzheimer’s patients with measurable damage to the brain regions that generate expectation lose the placebo component of pain relief specifically, and need higher real doses to get the same effect a healthy patient gets partly from ritual alone — which means the sickest, most cognitively impaired patients get the least benefit from the part of medicine that is free.55 Industry has tried to engineer around the opposite population — not people placebo doesn’t work on, but people it works on too well — screening candidates through a single-blind placebo “run-in” before randomization, meant to weed out placebo responders and widen the gap a drug needs to clear. A review of a hundred and one antidepressant trials found the run-in does neither: it does not lower placebo response after randomization, and it does not widen the drug-placebo gap.56 Even the industry’s one deliberate attempt to game its own control group does not work. And the scale of the whole enterprise, real as it is, resists a clean number: roughly one in eight interventional trials newly begun in a recent year listed a placebo intervention, out of tens of thousands registered that year alone, and the commercial market just for sourcing and manufacturing comparators — placebo included — is estimated in the low billions of dollars and growing, though no figure in that market is better sourced than a trade report.57

Look back over that evidence and a pattern sits in plain sight: the placebo works best when it costs the patient something. Swallowing beats nothing; being injected beats swallowing;7 a device beats a pill;8 a fake operation, complete with anesthesia and a scar, comes close enough to a real one that surgeons cannot always tell their patients apart from a placebo’s.9 The same story runs in reverse when the cost is framed as pain instead of relief: an expensive-labeled cream hurts more than a cheap one, tracked all the way to the spinal cord.41 Price alone does the work without a needle at all — the $2.50 pill beat the ten-cent pill by nearly twenty-five points.5 This is close to the only trend the formulation literature agrees on. The placebo that asks something of the patient — money, a needle, a scar — outperforms the placebo that asks nothing.

Regulators do not exploit this, because they are not allowed to want it. A trial control has to be, in the guidance’s own words, an “identically experienced” companion to the drug1 — a placebo pill for a pill, a placebo injection for an injection — because the entire purpose of the comparison is to isolate one variable, the molecule, and hold every other experience fixed. Build a more compelling control than the drug’s own presentation and you have not proven the drug does more than nothing; you have proven that an elaborate ritual beats a four-dollar tablet, which is true, uninteresting to a regulator, and would flunk every trial ever run past it. Assay sensitivity forecloses the very question this pattern raises: not whether the drug beats an inert copy of itself, but whether it beats the best thing we know how to do for this patient without it.

Nothing stops a health system from asking the second question anyway, outside the apparatus built to answer the first one. We already know the ritual is a dial, not a constant: turning up warmth, attention, and confidence around an identical sham took relief in irritable-bowel patients from twenty-eight percent to sixty-two, into the range of the condition’s actual drugs.22 And we know the dial does not require deceiving anyone to work. Patients told outright that their pills were placebo, “made of an inert substance, like sugar pills,” and shown to work anyway through what researchers called mind-body self-healing, still improved significantly more than patients getting nothing at all.58 Chronic low-back-pain patients given the same honest pitch on top of their usual care improved where usual care alone did not — and when the comparison group was later let in on the same open placebo, they improved too.59 If a treatment arm built entirely from warmth, ritual, and an honest description of itself can move outcomes on the order of what a real drug does, then a system that only ever benchmarks a drug against a deliberately unimpressive copy of itself is measuring the wrong contest. Ask drug makers to beat the best available treatment instead of an inert one; let a sufficiently well-designed placebo — honestly labeled, openly offered — count as that best treatment when it is; and the thing being optimized stops being only the molecule. It becomes the whole encounter: the warmth, the ritual, the story, the drug if one turns out to be needed. Call it behavioral science, since that is what it would be. It would not need to trick anyone to work — the open-label trials already show it doesn’t.

The honest cost of that reform is worth naming rather than waving off. A trial’s whole value lies in telling you why something worked, so the finding generalizes to the next patient and the next disease; a molecule that clears a well-specified biological target does that, and a masterfully warm clinician who cannot be mass-produced or patented does not, however well she does by the patient in the room. Benchmark everything against the best available ritual and medicine may get harder to tell apart from good bedside manner — a good outcome for the patient, and a much harder one to learn from, replicate, or scale. That tradeoff is worth having openly rather than by default, which is the position medicine is in now: nobody chose it, a regulation built to isolate one variable simply left the rest of the encounter on the table, and every clinician who is warm instead of businesslike is already running the experiment nobody funded, one patient at a time.
